# EY composition, data, and visual language

Use this Embedded PPT Master design reference after page content and semantic
Visual Direction are locked. It supplies a repertoire for the bounded Design
Lead and SVG Producer actions; it is not a content schema, wireframe,
coordinate plan, or set of additional PowerPoint Layouts. Keep concrete
composition choices out of `content.md` and preserve the bound
structured-template prototype.

## Design objective

Every authored Content page must make one approved claim or question visually
dominant and turn its supporting material into evidence, relationship, or
action. Do not treat the content-region as a container that must be evenly
filled. Use negative space, scale, alignment, contrast, and visual encoding to
create a clear first read, second read, and supporting read.

Use one composition family and one signature EY gesture per page. Do not
assemble a dashboard from independent cards. The dedicated Agenda Layout is
the only default card-grid exception.

## Composition families

Choose the family from the page's approved meaning, evidence shape, and Visual
Direction. These are internal authoring choices, not labels to render on the
slide.

### 1. Claim and evidence

Use when one conclusion is supported by two or three distinct proof points.
Let the claim dominate one visual field and organize the evidence as a
secondary sequence, not as equal cards. Prefer a shared baseline, progression,
or annotation system that reveals why the evidence belongs together.

Avoid a full-width bordered insight banner followed by evenly spaced text
columns. Integrate the claim into the composition.

### 2. Metric and trajectory

Use when a KPI, change, range, or time series is the argument. Make the data
mark and its direction the focal object; keep definitions, periods, and sources
supporting. Emphasize the approved endpoint, inflection, gap, or threshold
without inventing a new calculated label.

### 3. Comparative contrast

Use only for a real comparison: current/future, option A/B, segment X/Y,
expected/actual, or another approved contrast. Give both sides a common visual
measure so the difference is immediately legible. EY yellow identifies the
primary implication; blue may identify the genuine contrasting side.

### 4. Sequence and progression

Use for stages, chronology, cause-and-effect, or learning progression. Establish
one directional reading path with changes in position, scale, or connection.
Do not render a row of interchangeable process cards when the steps have a
meaningful dependency.

### 5. Relationship and ecosystem

Use when the page explains dependencies, flows, roles, or a system. Position
the approved focal entity or outcome as the anchor and use concise connectors,
proximity, and grouping to express the stated relationships. Do not connect
items that the approved content does not relate.

### 6. Matrix and portfolio

Use only when two approved dimensions support a placement, prioritization, or
coverage judgment. Make axes and zones meaningful. Avoid using a matrix merely
to arrange unrelated bullets or to create visual variety.

### 7. Image and argument

Use when a sourced or generated image materially improves comprehension of a
case, product, environment, stakeholder, or scenario. Treat the image as
evidence or context, crop it deliberately, and let the approved claim interact
with its negative space. Do not add decorative stock imagery to an analytical
page.

### 8. Decision and action

Use for recommendations, priorities, choices, owners, or next steps. Make the
decision or required action dominant, then show rationale, sequence, or
responsibility. Avoid giving every action identical visual weight when the
approved hierarchy indicates priority.

### 9. Reference table

Use a table only when the audience must look up or compare exact values across
multiple rows and columns. Create a clear scan path, restrained rules, direct
emphasis, and enough room for the approved labels. If the point is a trend,
ranking, gap, composition, or outlier, prefer a chart-led family instead.

When two families appear equally suitable, choose the one that best reveals
the approved relationship and differs from the immediately preceding authored
Content page when that page is available in context. Never force variety by
changing the meaning.

## Data-to-visual translation

Treat approved numbers as evidence to encode, not text to inventory. First
identify the question the data answers, then choose the simplest editable form
that makes the answer visible:

| Approved data relationship | Preferred visual encoding |
|---|---|
| Change over ordered time | line, slope, or aligned columns |
| Category magnitude or ranking | horizontal bar or dot plot |
| Two-value or two-period gap | paired bars, dumbbell, or slope |
| Part-to-whole | stacked bar; use a pie/donut only for a few stable parts |
| Range, target, or threshold | range bar, bullet treatment, or reference line |
| Contribution to a complete reconciliation | waterfall, only when all components are supplied |
| Relationship between two numeric variables | scatter, only with enough approved observations |
| Exact multi-field lookup | reference table |

For every chart-led composition:

1. Identify the one approved takeaway the chart must support.
2. Use one dominant series or comparison and visually subordinate context.
3. Direct-label the important data where approved labels exist; avoid a remote
   legend when direct labeling is possible.
4. Remove chart furniture that does not help interpretation: redundant axes,
   heavy borders, excessive gridlines, or repeated units.
5. Use yellow for the focal mark or implication, gray for context, and blue only
   for an approved contrast.
6. Keep data geometry proportional and truthful. Start quantitative bars at
   zero unless the chosen form and visible axis clearly communicate otherwise.
7. Keep chart titles, values, dates, units, caveats, and sources exactly bound
   to the visible-copy contract.

Geometry may encode differences derived directly from the approved values,
but do not render a new percentage, delta, ranking label, threshold, or
interpretation unless it already exists in the locked visible copy. Do not
turn table rows into disconnected text boxes that merely imitate a table.

## EY visual language

Use the following devices as a coherent brand language. Each page should use
one primary device confidently; combining all devices weakens the system.

### Directional beam

Extend the energy of the EY beam through a restrained diagonal edge, crop,
rule, wedge, or directional line. Use it to point toward the focal evidence,
separate a real contrast, or reinforce movement. Do not repeat miniature beam
motifs as decoration.

### Black field and negative space

Treat black as an active compositional field. Allow one object, chart, claim,
or image crop to occupy the visual center while surrounding space establishes
focus. Do not distribute small content blocks evenly across the entire canvas
just to fill it.

### Yellow mass and precision accents

Use EY yellow either as one decisive field or as a small number of precise
accents. A yellow field can carry a focal statement or frame a key comparison;
fine yellow rules can establish alignment or direction. Do not outline every
container or color every heading yellow.

### Asymmetry and crop

Prefer deliberate asymmetry, edge alignment, partial crop, and unequal visual
weight when they clarify hierarchy. Balance the page optically rather than
mirroring equal columns by default.

### Atmospheric color

Use the template's restrained glow or spectral color as depth and transition,
not as a generic background on every page. Keep it text-free, low-detail, and
subordinate to the approved content. Blue remains semantic contrast, not
ambient decoration.

### Lines, connectors, and boundaries

Prefer open alignment, hairlines, and meaningful connectors over enclosing
boxes. Use a boundary only when it communicates grouping, threshold, scope, or
comparison. Rounded rectangles, pills, badges, and repeated panels must not be
the default Content-page grammar.

### Images and icons

Use a small number of well-cropped, relevant images. Favor one strong image
over a collage. Use icons only when they carry recognizable semantic meaning;
do not place an icon above every heading as decoration.

## Candidate variety (advisory)

For a Standard page, a different suitable composition family or focal
mechanism can make A/B choice more useful. This is creative guidance only. It
is not a deterministic gate, and Independent Visual QA must not compare the
candidates or block either one for similarity. Both candidates preserve the
same approved meaning, exact visible copy, template contract, and data truth.
